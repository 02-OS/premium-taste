# Cereal

**Tags:** `editorial-print` `magazine` `photography` `serif` `space-as-hierarchy` `archive`

Two related objects: the magazine's print design, and the site at readcereal.com as redesigned by Studio Faculty. They teach the same idea at two sizes. This is the gallery's editorial-print reference.

## Description

Photography of places, interiors, and objects, printed or posted with very little chrome. In the Volume 16 print redesign, Studio Faculty kept a serif for most of the text and a sans for captions and credits, and they refused the usual magazine jump from a huge headline to tiny body. Hierarchy comes from space, from a ragged edge, and from a caption that is actually a caption. Dialogue can sit in italic. A rule can mark the end of a piece.

The 2023 site turned ten years of stories into an archive. Studio Faculty describe a header with almost no main navigation: search, information, and filters. The homepage gallery draws from a large set of photographs and can change between visits. The picture is the way you enter. The type does not restate the picture in a billboard.

## Key decisions

- Hierarchy by space and by role (serif prose, sans caption), not by shouting size (`type.scale-jumps` used with restraint).
- Ragged right, not justified, for reading.
- The photograph is full and quiet. It is not cropped into a card and it is not under a scrim with a centered headline.
- Navigation gets out of the way. Search and a filter are enough when the archive is the product.
- One italic voice for a real textual reason (speech), not as a second brand.
- The system is calm enough that a place can be the entire event of the page.

## Why it works

The product is a way of looking. A large, unhurried photograph and a column you can read accomplish that. A hero, three "pillars," and a newsletter card would argue with the photograph (`uniqueness.refuse-skeleton`). Space is doing a job: it keeps the title from competing with the picture, which is the editorial version of `hierarchy.three-levels`.

## What to steal

- A text serif for prose and a sans for captions, credits, and navigation.
- A measure you can read, ragged right, with captions clearly smaller and not trapped in a colored box.
- An image that is allowed to be the first screen, with a factual caption under it or beside it.
- Navigation as a few words or a search field when the content is an archive.
- The print lesson, translated carefully: if you do not need five type sizes, do not invent them. On screen, still keep body at a readable size (`a11y.size`). Their print body is small because it is print.

## What not to copy

- Emptiness as an aesthetic when you have no photographs. Cereal without pictures is not editorial. It is a blank (`density.not-empty`).
- A drop cap, a pull quote in a card, a huge headline, and a background texture, all at once.
- Justified text on the web. The rag was a deliberate readability choice.
- Their archive randomization as a gimmick on a store. It works because the archive is deep.
- A centered serif headline over every article image with a gradient scrim. That is the template version of "magazine."

## Uniqueness

Cereal publishes a look at a place. The writing and the photograph are the product, and hierarchy comes from space rather than from a front-page size ladder. Neighbors in editorial print: the Financial Times is one salmon sheet and a numeric daily; the New York Times ranks stories on a white front; Monocle is a denser sectioned briefing. Aesop, in quiet luxury, sells an object with label copy. Cereal does not teach rules, market columns, or a daily ranking.

## Product fit

Essays, magazines, interviews, travel and place, lookbooks that are actually written, catalog introductions.

A poor fit for a task list, a checkout, or an event listing. A schedule wants Resident Advisor's density, not a gallery wall.
